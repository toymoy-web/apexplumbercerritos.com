Why Does My Drain Keep Clogging After It's Been Snaked?

A drain that clogs again a few weeks after being cabled is one of the most common follow-up calls we get. Here's why that happens and what actually fixes it.

What A Cable Actually Does.

A drain cable, sometimes called a snake, works by cutting or punching a path through whatever's blocking a pipe. It's fast and effective for a lot of clogs, especially a localized hair clog or a single blockage close to a fixture, and it's usually the right first move.

A cable clears a path through the clog, not the full width of the pipe. Whatever caused the original blockage, whether that's grease coating the pipe walls or roots growing in through a joint, is often still there, lining the sides of the pipe around the hole the cable just cut through. That leftover material is exactly what causes the same spot to clog again before too long.

Think of it like clearing a hiking trail through overgrown brush. The path is walkable again, but the brush on either side hasn't gone anywhere, and it doesn't take long before it's crowding the same trail again, just like before.

Why It Comes Back.

If a drain clogs again within a few weeks of being cabled, the most common reasons are grease buildup that wasn't actually removed, just pushed aside, or root intrusion further down the line than the cable reached. Both continue narrowing the pipe from where the cable left off, and both eventually cause another blockage in roughly the same spot.

This shows up especially often on older cast-iron and clay lines, which describes a lot of the drain and sewer infrastructure in Cerritos and the surrounding cities. Decades of grease buildup or root intrusion in a line that's 40 to 60 years old isn't something a single cabling pass is likely to fully resolve.

There's also a timing pattern worth knowing: a clog that comes back within days usually means the cable didn't fully clear the original blockage. A clog that comes back weeks or months later, in the same general area, usually points to buildup regrowing on the pipe walls rather than a fresh, unrelated clog somewhere else in the system.

What Hydro Jetting Does Differently.

Hydro jetting uses high-pressure water to clear the full diameter of the pipe, not just a path through the center. It removes grease buildup, scale, and root intrusion along the entire section of line that's jetted, rather than leaving material behind for the next clog.

This is why jetting tends to hold up longer than repeated cabling on the same recurring problem. It's a different tool for a different situation: cabling for a first-time or simple clog, jetting for a line that's already shown it has a buildup problem a cable alone won't solve.

Jetting isn't the right first move for every clog, though. On pipe that's already cracked or structurally compromised, high-pressure water isn't a good idea until the line's condition has been checked, which is part of why a camera inspection sometimes comes before jetting rather than after. Where it is appropriate, jetting is a standard, code-recognized method under California Plumbing Code, not an aggressive or experimental technique.

When A Camera Inspection Makes Sense Too.

If a line has clogged more than once even after cabling, it's often worth a camera inspection alongside jetting, to see exactly what's in the line rather than guessing. A camera shows whether the issue is grease, root intrusion, a cracked joint, or a low spot in the pipe collecting debris, each of which points to a different long-term fix and a different repair cost.

Not every recurring clog needs a full inspection, but a line that's needed service more than twice in a short window is a reasonable point to stop repeating the same fix and find out what's actually happening inside the pipe.

A camera inspection also gives you something concrete to plan around. Instead of wondering whether the next service call is weeks or years away, you get an actual look at the line's condition and a realistic sense of what it will need going forward.

Preventing The Next Clog, Not Just Clearing This One.

Once a line's been jetted and any structural issues addressed, there are a few habits that genuinely slow down how fast buildup returns. Avoid pouring grease or oil down a kitchen drain, use a hair catcher in showers and tubs, and be cautious about what goes down a garbage disposal in a home with older drain lines.

None of that prevents every future clog on an aging line, but it does stretch the time between service calls. That matters more on a line that's already shown it has a buildup problem than on newer, wider PVC pipe that hasn't had the same decades of wear.

How Often A Line Like This Actually Needs Jetting.

There's no single answer that fits every home, but a line with a known grease or root intrusion problem in an older Cerritos property often benefits from jetting on a schedule, rather than waiting for the next clog to force the issue. Every year or two is a reasonable starting point for a line with a documented history of buildup, adjusted based on what a camera inspection actually shows.

A line that's never had a recurring problem doesn't need this kind of preventive schedule. Reactive cabling as clogs happen is perfectly reasonable for a healthy line, and jetting on a schedule is really a tool for a line that's already demonstrated it needs more than an occasional cable pass.

What To Say When You Call.

If you're calling about a repeat clog, mentioning that it's been snaked before and came back helps us come prepared with the right equipment for the job. Roughly how long it took to clog again is useful too, rather than us showing up with just a cable and having to reschedule for jetting on a separate visit.

If you already know or suspect the line is older cast-iron or clay, that's worth mentioning too. It doesn't change whether we can help, but it does shape what we bring and whether a camera inspection makes sense as part of the same visit, rather than scheduling it as a separate trip later on.

One Clog Isn't A Verdict On The Whole House.

A single clogged drain doesn't mean every line in the house has the same problem. Clogs are often localized to one fixture, one section of pipe, or one branch line, and a repeat problem in the kitchen doesn't necessarily say anything about the bathroom lines or the main sewer connection running under the yard.

That said, if more than one drain in the house has started acting up around the same time, that's a different pattern worth mentioning when you call. It points more toward a shared main line issue than a series of unrelated, isolated clogs happening to occur close together.
