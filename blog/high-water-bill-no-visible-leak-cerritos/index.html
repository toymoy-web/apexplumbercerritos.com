Why Is My Water Bill So High In Cerritos With No Visible Leak?

A bill that jumps with nothing dripping anywhere is one of the most common calls we get. Here's how to narrow down the cause before assuming it's just usage.

Start With The Meter, Not The House.

A high water bill with nothing dripping anywhere usually traces to one of three things. There's a hidden leak somewhere in the system, a genuine change in how much water the household used, or occasionally a utility rate increase that has nothing to do with your usage at all.

Before tearing apart cabinets or walking the yard looking for a wet spot, the water meter is the fastest way to confirm whether water is actually being lost. Most homes in Cerritos have a meter near the curb or sidewalk, sometimes under a small metal or plastic lid.

Turn off every fixture in the house, including anything automatic like an ice maker, a water softener regeneration cycle, or a sprinkler timer running on its own schedule. Note the meter reading, wait 20 to 30 minutes without using any water, then check it again. If the number moved, or if the meter has a small triangular or spinning low-flow indicator that's still turning, water is going somewhere it shouldn't.

Write the reading down rather than relying on memory, and take a photo of the meter face if you can. If you end up calling a plumber, having that exact reading and the time you took it gives a real data point to start from instead of a vague description.

The Usual Suspects, In Order.

A running toilet is the single most common cause of a high bill with no visible leak. A worn flapper lets water trickle from the tank into the bowl continuously, often silently enough that nobody notices. A few drops of food coloring in the tank, left for 10 minutes without flushing, will show up in the bowl if the flapper is leaking.

After toilets, irrigation systems are the next most common culprit in this area, especially a stuck valve or a cracked line feeding a sprinkler zone. A dripping outdoor hose bibb or an aging water softener stuck mid-cycle are also worth ruling out before assuming the problem is inside the house at all.

Appliance connections are another spot worth a quick look. The water line behind a washing machine and the supply line to a dishwasher both have fittings that can loosen or wear out over time. The connection under a refrigerator with an ice maker is another common spot, and any of these can leak a small, steady amount without ever pooling visibly.

If none of those check out and the meter still shows movement with everything off, the leak is likely underground or behind a wall, which is where a real diagnosis usually needs equipment rather than a flashlight and a guess.

Why Some Leaks Never Show A Puddle.

Cerritos sits on clay, sandy loam, and adobe soil that shifts with the seasons, and a lot of homes here still run original 1960s and 1970s supply lines. A pinhole leak in a copper line running through or under a slab foundation can leak for weeks or months without ever surfacing as a visible puddle, because the water is soaking into soil or concrete instead of pooling where you'd see it.

A warm spot on the floor, a slightly damp patch of carpet with no obvious source, or a section of yard that stays greener or wetter than the rest are all signs worth paying attention to, even without standing water.

The reason this matters more here than in some other areas is straightforward: older pipe material combined with soil that moves seasonally is a combination that produces exactly this kind of slow, hidden leak. It's not that Cerritos homes are unusually poorly built. It's that enough time has passed for original plumbing to start showing its age in a predictable way.

When It's Worth Calling A Plumber.

If the meter test confirms water is moving with everything off, and you've ruled out a running toilet and irrigation, that's the point where electronic leak detection makes sense rather than guessing at which wall or slab section to open. The equipment traces the leak's actual location first, so any repair is targeted instead of exploratory.

It's also worth calling sooner rather than later. A slow leak left unaddressed for months costs more in wasted water than the detection visit itself, and it gives the leak more time to affect flooring, foundation moisture, or a slab. Any repair that follows, from a fitting swap to a rerouted line, is done to California Plumbing Code standards.

One more thing worth mentioning: a sudden, dramatic spike is a different situation than a bill that's crept up gradually over several cycles. A sudden spike often points to a single new leak, while a gradual climb can point to something slower, like a fixture that's been failing a little more each month. Either way, the meter test is the same first step, and the follow-up conversation with a plumber goes faster once you already have that information in hand.

A Quick Reference Before You Call.

If you're picking up the phone, having a few details ready makes the call faster on both ends. The meter reading and how long you waited, whether the toilet dye test showed color, whether irrigation zones were checked, and roughly when the bill first jumped compared to your usual usage all help.

None of that is required before calling, and we're glad to walk through the checks with you over the phone if you haven't done them yet. But homeowners who've already ruled out the toilet and irrigation tend to get to the actual diagnosis faster, since we can skip straight to talking about equipment and scheduling instead of starting from scratch.

Seasonal Usage Vs. An Actual Leak.

It's worth ruling out ordinary seasonal change before assuming the worst. A first stretch of warm weather, a new lawn or landscaping that needs more frequent watering, house guests staying for a week, or simply more laundry and longer showers can all raise a bill without anything being broken.

The way to tell the difference is comparison, not guesswork. Look at the same billing period from a year earlier rather than just last month, since irrigation and outdoor use swing seasonally in a fairly predictable pattern. If this month looks similar to the same month last year, usage is the likely explanation. If it's meaningfully higher than the same period in prior years, that points more toward a leak than a seasonal shift. It's also reasonable to call and ask us to help interpret a bill if you're not sure which explanation fits, since we look at this pattern regularly across homes in the same area and can usually tell fairly quickly which direction it points.
