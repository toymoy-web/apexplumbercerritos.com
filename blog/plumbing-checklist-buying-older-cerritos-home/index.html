Buying An Older Cerritos Home? Check These Plumbing Items First

A standard home inspection covers a lot, but not everything worth genuinely knowing before you sign anything at all. Here's what's worth checking specifically before buying a home built in the 1960s or 1970s.

Ask About The Age Of The Supply Lines.

A home's age is a starting clue, but it doesn't tell you everything, since some homes have already had a partial or full repipe while others still run entirely original material. Ask directly whether the supply lines are original galvanized, original copper, or have been replaced, and if so, when and with what.

Original galvanized supply lines, common in the oldest homes in this area, are generally at or past the end of their practical service life and worth budgeting for replacement even without an active symptom yet.

Get A Sewer Camera Inspection.

This is the single item most likely to be missing from a standard inspection entirely, since it requires specialized equipment a general inspector doesn't carry. Given how much of Cerritos' sewer infrastructure, both city-owned and private laterals, is 40 to 60 years old, this is worth adding as its own line item rather than assuming it's covered.

A clean result is valuable information on its own. A result showing root intrusion or a cracked section gives you real numbers to negotiate with or budget around, rather than an unpleasant surprise after you've already moved in.

Check The Water Heater's Actual Age And Condition.

A water heater's manufacture date is usually printed on a sticker on the unit itself, often as part of the serial number. If it's already past 10 to 15 years old, budgeting for a near-term replacement is more realistic than hoping it lasts significantly longer.

Ask whether it's ever been flushed, since a unit that's never had sediment cleared in a hard water area like this one may already be operating less efficiently than its age alone would suggest.

Ask About Any Past Leaks Or Repairs.

Sellers are often required to disclose known issues, but the level of detail varies, and a direct conversation can surface more than a checkbox disclosure form does. Ask specifically about slab leaks, water heater failures, or sewer backups, since these are exactly the kind of issues common in a home of this age and type in this area.

If a repair was made, ask whether it was a full fix or a patch, and if documentation exists. A previous slab leak that was spot-repaired rather than addressed with a reroute, for example, carries a real chance of recurring on the same aging line.

Test Water Pressure And Check For Backflow Devices.

Low pressure throughout the house, tested at a few different fixtures, can point to a restricted or aging main service line worth investigating before finalizing a purchase. If the property has an irrigation system, confirm whether a backflow preventer is installed and when it was last tested.

None of this needs to be a deal-breaker for a home you otherwise want. It's information that lets you negotiate fairly, plan a realistic budget for the first few years of ownership, and avoid being surprised by something that was, in hindsight, predictable given the home's age.

Putting This Checklist Into Practice.

You don't need to schedule five separate visits for this. A single pre-purchase plumbing assessment can typically cover the water heater check, a visual review of accessible supply lines, a water pressure test, and a discussion of what the sewer camera inspection found, all in one visit.

The goal isn't to find a reason to walk away from an otherwise good home. It's to walk into the purchase with accurate information rather than assumptions, so any negotiation or post-purchase budgeting is based on what's actually there rather than what an older home's age alone might suggest to a buyer working from general impressions.

A Final Note On Timing.

Whichever items from this list you choose to pursue, timing them within your inspection contingency period matters. That window is when findings actually give you leverage, whether that means requesting repairs, negotiating a credit, or simply making a fully informed decision to move forward with the purchase as planned.

A Note On Newer Homes In The Same Area.

This checklist is aimed at homes from the 1960s and 1970s specifically, but even a newer home in Cerritos can share some of these considerations if it's had additions, renovations, or plumbing work done using materials or practices from an earlier era. Age on paper isn't always a perfect proxy for what's actually installed.

If a listing mentions a partial remodel or addition, it's worth asking specifically whether the plumbing in that section matches the rest of the home or was updated separately. A mix of old and new materials on the same property is more common than buyers sometimes expect.

What This Actually Costs To Check Upfront.

A combined pre-purchase plumbing assessment, covering the items in this checklist, typically costs a few hundred dollars, a small fraction of a home purchase price and often less than a single unexpected repair discovered after moving in. Compared to the cost of skipping this step entirely and hoping for the best, it's one of the more straightforward investments in the whole buying process.

Where To Start If This Feels Like A Lot.

If none of this was mentioned to you during the buying process so far, start with the two highest-value items: a sewer camera inspection and a water heater age check. Both are quick, inexpensive, and address the two issues most likely to surprise a new owner of an older Cerritos home within the first year.

After You've Moved In.

Even with a thorough pre-purchase check, it's worth locating your main water shutoff valve and gas meter shutoff in your first week in a new home, before you ever need them in an actual emergency. Knowing this ahead of time is one of the simplest things a new owner can do, and it costs nothing but a few minutes of attention.

The Takeaway For Any Older Home Purchase.

An older home's plumbing isn't a reason to walk away. It's simply a system with a known age profile, and knowing what that age actually means for your specific property turns an unknown into a manageable, budgeted part of ownership rather than a recurring surprise.
