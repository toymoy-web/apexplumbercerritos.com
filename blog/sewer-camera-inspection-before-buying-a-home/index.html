Should You Get A Sewer Camera Inspection Before Buying An Older Cerritos Home?

A standard home inspection doesn't look inside the sewer line. Here's why that gap matters more in a city with as much aging infrastructure as Cerritos, and what a camera inspection actually finds.

Why A Standard Home Inspection Misses This.

A general home inspection covers visible, accessible systems: the roof, electrical panel, visible plumbing fixtures, the water heater, and so on around the property. It does not include running a camera down the sewer line, because that requires specialized equipment and isn't part of a standard inspection's scope, no matter how thorough and experienced the inspector happens to be at their job and their trade.

That means a home can pass a full inspection with a sewer line that's already cracked, partially collapsed, or full of root intrusion, simply because nobody looked. The first sign of a real problem is often a backup, sometimes within the first year of ownership.

This isn't a flaw in the standard inspection process. It's simply outside what a general inspector is equipped or expected to check, the same way a general inspector doesn't test a roof's remaining lifespan with specialized equipment either. A sewer camera inspection is a separate, optional add-on for exactly this reason.

Why This Matters More In Cerritos Specifically.

City reporting shows that roughly 70 percent of Cerritos' water pipelines were installed between 1966 and 1974, and much of the broader water and sewer network is now 40 to 60 years old across the city. Clay and cast-iron laterals in that age range are exactly the kind of pipe that root intrusion and joint separation target over time.

That doesn't mean every older Cerritos home has a sewer problem. It means the odds of an issue existing, without any visible sign from inside the house, are meaningfully higher here than in a neighborhood built out in the last couple of decades with modern PVC pipe throughout.

The same pattern shows up in neighboring cities with a similar building era, which is worth keeping in mind if you're comparing homes across Cerritos and the surrounding area rather than just one specific address.

What The Camera Actually Shows.

A sewer camera inspection shows the real, current condition of the line: root intrusion at joints, cracks or breaks, bellies where the pipe has sagged and started collecting standing water, and the general material and condition of the pipe itself throughout its length.

Each of those findings points to a different level of concern and a different likely cost. A small amount of root intrusion that can be cleared and monitored is a very different situation from a line with multiple breaks or a significant belly. The inspection is what tells you which one you're actually dealing with, rather than leaving it to guesswork after the sale closes.

You can also get a copy of the recorded footage, which is useful beyond the immediate purchase decision. It becomes a baseline record you can refer back to years later if a new symptom shows up, rather than starting from zero on a line you've never actually seen inside or had documented in any way.

How To Use The Findings In A Purchase Decision.

If an inspection turns up a real issue, that's useful information either way, whatever you ultimately decide to do about it. You can use it to negotiate a repair credit, ask the seller to address it before closing, or simply go in with clear eyes about a cost you'll likely face in the near term rather than being surprised by it after you've already moved in.

If the inspection comes back clean, that's valuable too. It means you're not inheriting someone else's deferred maintenance on a system that would otherwise be invisible until it fails.

Either way, a few hundred dollars for a camera inspection is a small price relative to a home purchase, and a much smaller one than an emergency sewer repair discovered after you've already moved in. Scheduling it during the standard inspection contingency period, rather than after closing, is what actually gives you leverage to act on what's found.

What This Costs Compared To What It Protects.

A standard residential sewer camera inspection runs a few hundred dollars, depending on line length and how many access points are involved. Compare that to the cost of an emergency sewer repair, which can run well into the thousands depending on scope, and the inspection is a small, one-time cost against a much larger potential expense.

It's also worth remembering that a clean inspection isn't wasted money even if nothing's found. Confirming a line is in good condition is valuable information on its own, whether you're buying the home or simply deciding it's not something to worry about for the next several years.

Who Actually Orders The Inspection.

In most cases, a buyer arranges the sewer camera inspection directly, separate from the general home inspector, since it requires different equipment and expertise most general inspectors don't carry on their trucks day to day. Some general inspectors can coordinate this as an add-on service, while others will simply recommend a plumber for it, which is worth asking about upfront.

Timing matters here. Scheduling it early enough in the inspection contingency period, rather than as an afterthought right before closing, gives you room to actually act on what's found. That might mean renegotiating, requesting repairs, or simply making an informed decision to move forward with the purchase as planned.

What If You're Selling Instead Of Buying.

The same logic runs in reverse for sellers. Getting a sewer camera inspection done before listing an older Cerritos home can surface an issue early enough to address it on your own terms, rather than having it come up during a buyer's inspection and become a negotiating point against you.

A clean inspection report can also be a genuine selling point for an older home, especially one competing against newer construction where buyers may already be uneasy about aging infrastructure. Being able to hand over recent footage showing a sound line removes one specific worry from the conversation.

A Reasonable Cadence For A Home You're Keeping.

Outside of a purchase or sale, there's no strict rule for how often an older home's sewer line needs a fresh camera inspection. A reasonable approach is to get one done once and establish a baseline. From there, repeat it every five to ten years for a home with clay or cast-iron laterals, or sooner if a new symptom like a slow drain or a recurring clog shows up in the meantime.

Think of it less as a fixed maintenance schedule and more as periodically checking in on a system that's genuinely aging, so a small, manageable issue gets caught before it becomes a full backup that disrupts the whole house.
