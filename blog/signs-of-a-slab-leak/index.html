How To Tell If You Have A Slab Leak

A slab leak rarely announces itself with an obvious puddle. Here's what to look for, and what to do once you suspect one.

What A Slab Leak Actually Is.

A slab leak is a leak in a water line running through or beneath a home's concrete slab foundation, rather than in an exposed pipe you can see and reach easily. Most homes built in the 1960s and 1970s, which describes a large share of Cerritos, have copper supply lines running through the slab, and decades of use combined with shifting soil is a common reason those lines eventually develop pinhole leaks.

Because the leak is under concrete, it doesn't behave like a normal leak. Water spreads sideways under the slab, sometimes surfacing far from where the actual leak is, which is part of why guessing at a repair location rarely works out well.

It's also worth knowing that a slab leak can be on the hot side or the cold side of the plumbing system. A hot-side leak tends to show up as a warm spot and, over time, a noticeably higher water heating cost, while a cold-side leak is more likely to show up first as a rising water bill with no obvious temperature change at all.

The Signs Worth Paying Attention To.

A warm spot on the floor, especially over a hot water line, is one of the clearest signs. If you walk across a tile or hardwood floor and notice one area feels warmer than the rest with no obvious explanation, that's always worth investigating further.

A water bill that's climbed for a month or two with no change in household habits is another common sign, especially paired with a water meter that keeps turning when every fixture in the house is off.

Water showing up at the edge of a slab, often near a garage or an exterior wall, is another pattern we see often in Cerritos homes. The water travels along the path of least resistance under the slab and surfaces wherever it finds an opening, which is frequently at a perimeter edge rather than directly above the actual leak.

Less commonly, a musty smell in one part of the house, a soft or slightly spongy feeling in flooring, or the sound of running water when everything is off can also point to a slab leak somewhere nearby. Cracking in a slab or nearby drywall that wasn't there before, especially if it appears somewhat suddenly, is worth mentioning to a plumber even if it seems more like a structural question at first glance.

What Not To Do First.

It's tempting to start guessing at a repair location based on where symptoms show up, but because water travels under a slab before surfacing, opening concrete based on a guess often misses the actual leak entirely and creates unnecessary damage.

Electronic and acoustic leak detection equipment is built specifically for this problem. It locates the leak's real position before any concrete work is discussed, so a repair plan is based on where the leak actually is, not where the symptom happened to show up.

It's also worth resisting the urge to shut off water to the whole house for an extended period while you decide what to do, unless the leak is severe enough to be an emergency. A confirmed slow slab leak is rarely an immediate crisis, and a scheduled detection visit usually beats an emergency call in terms of both cost and convenience.

Repair Or Reroute, And How That Gets Decided.

Once a slab leak is located, there are generally two paths forward. One is a direct spot repair, which opens a small section of slab right at the leak. The other is a reroute, which runs a new line above the slab or through a wall or attic instead of repairing the section under the concrete.

A single, isolated leak in an otherwise sound line often makes sense as a spot repair. A line that's already had multiple leaks, or one that's simply reached the end of its expected service life, is often better addressed with a reroute, since it avoids the risk of another slab leak on the same aging line a year or two later.

Cost, flooring type, and how the rest of the home's plumbing is laid out all factor into which option makes more sense for a given house. A straight comparison of both options, with real numbers for your specific situation, is worth asking for before deciding. Whichever approach fits, the repair itself follows California Plumbing Code standards for pipe material and routing.

How Long Does A Slab Leak Repair Actually Take.

A spot repair is usually a one-day job in straightforward cases: locate the leak, open a small section of slab, repair the line, and patch the opening. Flooring replacement, if the existing floor covering needs to be removed and redone, adds time on top of the plumbing work itself.

A reroute can sometimes be completed just as quickly, since it avoids breaking concrete entirely, though running a new line through an attic or wall may take longer depending on the home's layout and how accessible the space is.

Either way, the detection visit itself is quick, usually well under a day, and it's the step that turns a vague worry into an actual plan with a realistic price and repair timeline attached.

Can A Slab Leak Cause Bigger Problems If You Wait.

A slow slab leak isn't usually an emergency in the way a burst pipe is, but waiting has real costs. Ongoing moisture under a slab can affect flooring, especially wood or laminate, and can contribute to musty odors or mold in enclosed spaces near the leak over time.

There's also the water bill itself. A leak that goes unaddressed for months, even a fairly small one, adds up to real money in wasted water, often more than the cost of the detection and repair combined would have been if handled earlier.

None of this means every warm spot is a five-alarm emergency. It means a suspected slab leak is worth scheduling a detection visit for sooner rather than treating it as something to think about later, once it becomes more convenient.

Insurance And Documentation.

Coverage for a slab leak varies by policy, and it's worth checking your homeowner's policy before assuming either way. Some policies cover the cost of accessing and repairing the leak itself but not the resulting damage, while others draw the line differently between the two categories entirely.

Whatever your policy says, keeping documentation helps. We can provide a written summary of what the detection equipment found and what the repair involved, which is the kind of record an insurer typically wants to see if you do end up filing a claim, rather than a verbal description after the fact.
