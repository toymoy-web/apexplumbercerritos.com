Sump Pump Maintenance: What Homeowners Should Actually Know

A sump pump that's never checked between emergencies is a pump you're hoping works, not one you actually know works for certain. Here's what real maintenance looks like in practice.

Why Maintenance Matters More Than People Assume.

A sump pump typically sits unused for long stretches, especially outside the rainy season, and a pump that isn't run periodically can develop a stuck float switch or a seized motor without anyone knowing until the moment it's actually needed.

The worst time to discover a pump has failed is during an active flooding event, when there's no time left to troubleshoot before real damage occurs. Routine maintenance exists specifically to catch problems while there's still time to fix them calmly.

A Basic Check You Can Do Yourself.

Pour a bucket of water into the sump pit to raise the water level and confirm the pump activates, moves water through the discharge line, and shuts off properly once the level drops. This simple test, done every few months, catches a surprising number of developing issues.

Visually check the discharge line outside the home to confirm it's actually routing water away from the foundation, not pooling right back near the house. A discharge line that empties too close to the foundation can effectively recirculate the same water it just pumped away.

What A Professional Check Adds.

A technician checks the float switch's full range of motion, the check valve that prevents water from flowing backward into the pit between cycles, and the pump's overall condition in a way that's harder to assess with a simple bucket test alone.

If your pump doesn't have a battery backup and your area has experienced power outages during past storms, this is also a natural point to discuss adding one, since a primary pump alone is only as reliable as the power supply keeping it running.

How Often This Actually Needs Doing.

A simple bucket test every few months, plus a full professional check once a year, generally before the rainy season starts, is a reasonable baseline for most homes. A crawlspace with a documented history of water intrusion may warrant checking more frequently.

If a pump has run continuously through a significant storm event, it's worth a follow-up check afterward specifically, since sustained heavy use is exactly the kind of stress that can reveal wear that a quick test under normal conditions wouldn't have caught.

Signs Something's Already Wrong.

A pump that's noticeably louder than it used to be, or one that runs for unusually long cycles, is worth having checked. The same is true of one that's started running more frequently without an obvious change in conditions, rather than waiting for a full failure to force the issue.

A musty smell developing in a crawlspace that previously didn't have one, even without visible standing water, can indicate a pump that's no longer keeping up with normal moisture levels the way it used to.

Keeping A Simple Record.

Note the date each time you run the bucket test or have a professional check completed, along with anything notable, like an unusually long cycle time or a sound that seemed off. This takes almost no effort but gives you and any technician a real baseline to compare against if something does eventually change.

If you've just moved into a home with an existing sump pump and have no history on it at all, treat the first check as establishing that baseline rather than assuming everything is fine simply because it hasn't obviously failed yet.

What A Neglected Pump Actually Costs You.

The cost of routine maintenance is minor compared to the cost of water damage from a pump that failed silently during an actual flooding event. Drying and restoring a flooded crawlspace, along with any affected flooring or structure above it, typically costs far more than years of simple bucket tests and annual professional checks combined.

Thinking of maintenance as insurance against that much larger cost, rather than an optional extra, is the more accurate way to weigh whether it's worth the small amount of time it actually takes.

Checking The Backup Battery, Not Just The Pump.

If your system already has a battery backup, that battery itself needs periodic attention. Most backup batteries have a useful life of a few years before they can no longer hold enough charge to run the pump through an extended outage, even though the battery might appear fine sitting unused.

A technician can test the actual backup runtime under load, which tells you far more than simply confirming the battery shows a charge. A battery can hold voltage while still being too degraded to power the pump for more than a few minutes during a real outage.

What To Do If You're Moving Into A Home With An Existing System.

If a home you're buying or have just moved into already has a sump pump installed, don't assume it's in good working order simply because it's there. Ask the previous owner directly about its age, whether it's ever failed or been serviced, and whether it includes a battery backup.

If that information isn't available, treat the pump as an unknown quantity and have it assessed early, ideally before the next rainy season rather than during it, so you have a real baseline and enough time to address anything that needs attention. Any new installation follows California Plumbing Code standards for discharge routing.

The Simplest Summary.

Test it with the bucket method every few months. Have it professionally checked once a year, ideally before the rainy season. Check the discharge line's actual path outside. And if you have a battery backup, confirm it can genuinely hold a charge under load, not just that it shows one. Four simple habits, none of them time-consuming, are what separate a pump you can actually count on from one you're simply hoping works when it matters most in a real emergency.

A Closing Thought.

None of this requires specialized knowledge or expensive tools on your end. It requires remembering to actually do it on a schedule, which is the part that tends to slip once the rainy season passes and the pump goes back to sitting quietly, doing nothing, until the next time it's actually needed.

The Takeaway.

A sump pump you've actually tested recently is a real safety net. One you've never checked is simply an assumption, and the difference between those two only becomes obvious at the exact moment you can least afford it to matter, during an actual storm.

If You're Not Sure Where To Start.

We can run a full system check, including the float switch, discharge line, and any existing battery backup. That gives you a clear picture of where things stand rather than leaving you to guess about it or find out the hard way during the next storm.
