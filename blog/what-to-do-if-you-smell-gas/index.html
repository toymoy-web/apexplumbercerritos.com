What To Do When You Smell Gas In Your Home

A gas smell is one of the few plumbing-adjacent situations that calls for immediate, specific action. Here's exactly what that looks like.

Before We Arrive.

Leave the house immediately if you smell gas, and get everyone else out with you. Don't stop to gather belongings or investigate the source first.

Do not operate any light switches, appliances, phones, or anything electrical inside the home, including turning lights on or off. A spark from an electrical switch can ignite accumulated gas.

Do not use a lighter, match, or any open flame to check for the source. This should go without saying, but it's worth stating plainly because the instinct to investigate is common.

Once safely outside, call from a distance, ideally from a neighbor's house or from the street rather than right next to the building. If you know where your gas meter's shutoff valve is and can reach it safely without going back inside, shutting it off is reasonable. Otherwise, wait for help.

What A Gas Smell Usually Means.

Natural gas itself is odorless. Utility companies add a chemical called mercaptan, which smells like sulfur or rotten eggs, specifically so leaks are noticeable. If you're smelling that distinctive odor, something in the system, whether that's a fitting, an appliance connection, or the line itself, is allowing gas to escape where it shouldn't.

A faint, occasional smell near an appliance is still worth having checked, even if it doesn't seem urgent. It's easier to address a small, developing issue than to wait until it becomes a stronger, more dangerous leak.

Common Sources Of A Leak.

Appliance connections are one of the most common sources, particularly a flexible connector that's aged, kinked, or wasn't properly tightened during a past appliance swap. A stove, dryer, or water heater connection are all worth checking.

Aging gas lines themselves, especially in a home with original 1960s or 1970s piping, can develop small leaks at joints over decades of minor ground settling and thermal expansion and contraction.

A disturbed underground line, sometimes from nearby digging or landscaping work, can also cause a leak that wasn't there before a specific event, which is worth mentioning if you know recent work happened near a buried line.

After The Immediate Danger Passes.

Once you or your gas utility have confirmed it's safe to return, don't re-enter until you've been told it's clear. A technician can then locate the specific source of the leak using proper equipment, rather than everyone guessing based on where the smell seemed strongest.

Repairing the actual leak, whether that's an appliance connector, a fitting, or a section of line, is a job for a qualified technician. This isn't an area where a confident guess and a wrench are a reasonable substitute for the real diagnosis.

Preventing The Next One.

Appliance connectors have a service life and are worth replacing proactively during any appliance swap rather than reusing an old one to save a small amount of money. A technician can also do a general check of accessible gas connections during an unrelated visit if you'd like peace of mind between issues.

If your home has original gas lines from the 1960s or 1970s, it's not unreasonable to have them assessed generally, especially if you're planning a kitchen remodel, an outdoor kitchen addition, or a tankless water heater conversion that will add demand to the existing system.

Gas Detectors As An Early Warning.

A natural gas detector, similar in concept to a smoke detector, can alert a household to a leak before the smell alone would be noticeable, particularly overnight or in a part of the home that isn't regularly occupied. They're inexpensive relative to the peace of mind they provide.

A detector doesn't replace the need to leave immediately if you smell gas directly, but it adds another layer of warning for a slow leak that might otherwise go unnoticed for longer than it should.

What Happens Once Help Arrives.

A technician or utility responder uses a gas detection meter to trace the leak's exact source rather than relying on smell alone, which can be misleading about the precise location, especially outdoors or in a larger space.

Once the source is confirmed, the repair itself, whether that's tightening or replacing a fitting, swapping an appliance connector, or repairing a section of line, is straightforward for a qualified technician. The investigation and safety precautions beforehand are what actually take the most care.

Why This Deserves More Caution Than Most Plumbing Issues.

Most plumbing problems, even urgent ones like a burst pipe, don't carry a risk of fire or explosion. A gas leak is different, which is why the standard advice is to leave first and investigate never, rather than the more typical approach of assessing a problem before deciding how urgently to respond.

This is also why we treat any call mentioning a gas smell as a priority, dispatching a technician as quickly as possible rather than fitting it into a normal scheduling queue alongside routine plumbing work.

A Note On Older Cerritos Homes Specifically.

Homes from the 1960s and 1970s sometimes have gas lines that predate current material and installation standards, even if they've never shown an obvious problem. That doesn't mean every older line is a hidden hazard, but it does mean a general assessment is a reasonable thing to schedule proactively, particularly before adding a new gas appliance or increasing overall demand on the system.

If you've never had your home's gas lines inspected and don't know their age or material, that's common, and it's a straightforward thing to have checked during an otherwise unrelated visit rather than requiring a dedicated appointment.

The Bottom Line On Gas Safety.

The instructions here are simple on purpose: leave, don't create a spark, and call from a safe distance. Overthinking the situation or trying to investigate before help arrives is where real risk comes from, not from the leak itself in the short time it takes to get outside.

Everything else, finding the source, repairing it, and confirming the home is safe again, is a job for a qualified technician with the right equipment, not a homeowner working from a guess about where the smell seemed strongest.

One Last Reminder Worth Repeating.

If you're ever unsure whether a smell is strong enough to count as an emergency, treat it as one every single time. The cost of an unnecessary call is nothing compared to the risk of waiting on a genuine leak, and no technician will ever fault you for calling about a smell that turned out to be minor in the end.

Keep This Guide Somewhere Handy.

The steps here are simple, but panic makes simple things harder to remember in the moment. Keeping a note with your gas utility's number and your own address near a phone, or saved in your phone itself, means you're not trying to recall or look anything up while you're already outside and anxious.
